\begin{abstract}
\noindent
(could use a rewrite)
When language models are aligned to human preferences, the moral
disagreements between annotators are averaged away, and with them the
principled differences between ethical traditions. This dissertation asks
what changes when those traditions are instead kept apart and combined by
an explicit rule rather than by implicit averaging. Sixteen
candidate responses to each of $500$ everyday moral dilemmas are scored
by three language-model judges, one utilitarian, one deontological, and
one Ubuntu, whose fidelity to their frameworks is tested behaviourally
on hand-built axioms rather than assumed. The scores are combined under
four aggregation methods from the moral uncertainty literature: expected
choiceworthiness, maximin, a Nash bargain, and a single-theory
utilitarian baseline. The rules prefer
different responses on $32\%$ of dilemmas and recommend different
actions on $11.2\%$, a strict lower bound that no tie-breaking
convention could reduce. Additionally, it is shown that the divergence concentrates where the
frameworks conflict; on the quarter of dilemmas where the judges disagree
most, action-level divergence reaches $27\%$, four times the rate
elsewhere. This suggests that the choice of aggregation method matters precisely when moral
uncertainty is real. Finally, the three judges are distilled into small
open models. The copies beat every prompted and statistical baseline on
fidelity to their teachers, and a full re-run of the pipeline on their
scores reproduces each aggregate conclusion of the study. The finer
grain resists copying. The copies recover less than half of the ranking
the teacher reliably repeats, and on behavioural axiom tests they barely
improve on their untrained base model. A correlation of $0.84$ would
conventionally certify such a copy as faithful. The behavioural tests
show how much that certificate leaves out. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

As AI systems become increasingly capable and influential, the problem of
ensuring they behave in accordance with human values grows correspondingly
urgent. The alignment problem \cite{christian2020} is the task of ensuring
that an AI system pursues the goals and honours the values its developers
and users intend, rather than proxies that merely correlate with them. In
practice, the dominant approach to aligning large language models is
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) \cite{ouyang2022}, in
which human raters compare model outputs and their preferences are
distilled into a reward signal that guides fine-tuning.

These raters often disagree with one another, particularly in normatively
ambiguous moral dilemmas, where there is no universal consensus on right or
wrong. Crucially, these disagreements do not stem merely from personal
idiosyncrasy or noise; they reflect principled differences between
competing ethical frameworks. A utilitarian, a Kantian, and an Ubuntu
thinker may each reach a different conclusion while reasoning coherently
from within their own tradition. In RLHF, this disagreement is not
preserved, however. A single reward model is trained to fit the aggregate
of many annotators' conflicting preferences, converging on a signal that
reflects the pool's majority tendency and offers no explicit account of how
competing moral views ought to be combined \cite{casper2023}.

This dissertation investigates whether the choice of moral
aggregation method produces measurably different LLM behaviour when applied
to normatively ambiguous dilemmas. Drawing on \citeauthor{macaskill2020}'s
formal framework for decision-making under moral uncertainty
\cite{macaskill2020}, I implement three aggregation schemes (Expected
Choiceworthiness, Maximin, and Nash Moral Parliament) as competing
response-selection criteria for a language model, alongside a single-theory
utilitarian baseline. Candidate responses are scored by three ethical
reward models operationalising three distinct moral traditions, namely
utilitarian, deontological, and Ubuntu. I test whether different
aggregation methods select systematically different responses, and if so,
where and why the methods diverge. This question has growing practical
importance; as users increasingly turn to language models for guidance on
sensitive personal and ethical decisions, the implicit moral character of
these systems is no longer a theoretical concern. It is a design choice with
real consequences.

Two things are held fixed throughout , the pool of candidate responses and
the judges that score it \textbf{huh? the judges change though? like with the Qwen models}, like h), so that any difference in outcome is
attributable to the aggregation rule and to nothing else. The question
then divides in two, and the halves are deliberately distinct. Two rules
may prefer different texts that steer a reader toward the same course of
action, which is disagreement over justification. Or they may prefer
texts whose recommendations are opposed, which is disagreement over what
ought to be done. The design measures both
(Section~\ref{sec:divergence}).

The dissertation makes three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{An end-to-end pipeline for moral-uncertainty aggregation.}
  Five hundred everyday dilemmas filtered for cross-framework tension, each
  with sixteen candidate responses. Three framework judges score all
  $8{,}000$ responses, and four selection rules are applied to the
  normalised scores, with Ubuntu included on equal terms with the two
  Western traditions (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item \textbf{A behavioural validation method for framework judges.} Each
  judge is tested against fifteen operational axioms drawn from the
  normative literature, with directional predictions for the other two
  judges and a confirmation set guarding against prompt overfitting
  (Section~\ref{sec:axiom-validation}). The harness is reusable for
  certifying any purported framework judge.
  \item \textbf{A two-level, tie-robust measure of divergence.} Selections
  are treated as winner sets rather than broken by arbitrary tie-breaks,
  and disagreement over the chosen text is reported separately from
  disagreement over the recommended action, so that no reported
  disagreement could be removed by a choice of tie-break
  (Section~\ref{sec:divergence}).
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:background} sets out the formal problem and the
aggregation proposals this study operationalises,
Section~\ref{sec:related} situates the work in the surrounding literature,
and Sections~\ref{sec:method} and~\ref{sec:results} describe the pipeline
and its results.

\section{Moral Uncertainty}
\label{sec:background}

Moral uncertainty is uncertainty about which moral theory is correct, as
distinct from uncertainty about the facts. An agent may know exactly what an
action will cause, yet still not know whether outcomes are all that morally
matters. Formally, the agent faces options $A$ and entertains rival theories
$T_1, \dots, T_n$ with credences $w_1, \dots, w_n$ summing to one, each
grading the options with a real-valued choiceworthiness function $c_i$. The
problem is which option is rational to choose, given credences and
verdicts together \cite{macaskill2020}. The sceptical reply, "act on the
theory you find most plausible", ignores stakes, just as acting on the
most probable state of the world ignores stakes under empirical
uncertainty. A theory held at credence $0.4$ that mildly favours an
option should not silence theories with combined credence $0.6$ that
condemn it as catastrophic \cite{macaskill2020}.

The standard constructive proposal, maximising \textbf{expected
choiceworthiness} (MEC), imports the apparatus of expected utility
\cite{lockhart2000,macaskill2020}:
\[
  a^{\star} \;=\; \arg\max_{a \in A}\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\, c_i(a).
\]
MEC presupposes that the $c_i$ are cardinal and expressed in a common unit,
though the theories themselves supply neither. There is no theory-neutral answer
to how many units of utilitarian welfare a broken Kantian duty is worth.
This is the \emph{intertheoretic value problem}, the central obstacle to
any additive rule \cite{macaskill2020}. The usual response is
normalisation, which places the theories on a common scale by statistical
fiat, whether by equalising each theory's range \cite{lockhart2000} or
its variance \cite{cottonbarratt2020}, and over the present options alone
or over everything a theory could ever grade. Both problem and responses
recur here, as the judges differ in how much of the $0$--$10$ scale they
use, and the global $z$-scoring of Section~\ref{sec:zscore} is a
broad-scope variance normalisation.

MEC also inherits the pathologies of expected utility, chief among them
\emph{fanaticism}. Because credence and stakes are multiplied, a theory
held at very low credence can still dominate the sum whenever it
declares the stakes extreme enough \cite{macaskill2020}. That worry
motivates rules that do not add. \citet{bostrom2009} pictures the
theories as a \textbf{moral parliament}, delegates who hold seats in
proportion to credence and negotiate a compromise, a proposal later
formalised by \citet{newberry2021}. The parliament is a picture rather
than a rule, and bargaining theory supplies the missing mechanism. The
\textbf{Nash solution} \cite{nash1950}, applied to moral uncertainty by
\citet{greaves2019}, selects the option that maximises the product of
each theory's gain over a disagreement point, rewarding compromise,
since an option that leaves any one theory near its disagreement point
has its product destroyed however well the others fare. Finally, a
risk-averse agent may prefer to protect the worst-served
theory rather than maximise an average, which yields \textbf{maximin},
grading each option by the lowest choiceworthiness any theory assigns
it. Where MEC asks which option is best on balance, maximin asks which
option the most dissatisfied theory can best live with.
Section~\ref{sec:rules} instantiates one member of each family,
alongside a single-theory \textbf{utilitarian baseline} for a system
that ignores moral uncertainty altogether.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}


Computational treatments of moral uncertainty are recent and sparse.
\citet{ecoffet2021} give the first reinforcement-learning
implementation, encoding rival theories as reward functions in
gridworlds, and identify intertheoretic comparison as the immediate
practical obstacle. \citet{tennant2024} fine-tune language-model agents
on intrinsic rewards encoding a utilitarian or a deontological
objective, one tradition at a time. More recent work operationalises
moral-uncertainty frameworks for decision support \cite{rosello2025},
or studies how uncertainty about values surfaces in human and model
judgments \cite{kwon2025}. A parallel line aggregates across people
rather than theories, whether through social choice over diverse human
feedback \cite{conitzer2024} or the voting-based system distilled from
the Moral Machine experiment \cite{awad2018,noothigattu2018}. None of
this work compares the philosophical aggregation rules as selection
criteria over natural-language advice, which is the setting studied
here.

The alignment literature has begun to treat value disagreement as a
problem in its own right. \citet{gabriel2020} distinguishes the
technical problem of fitting a system to given values from the
normative problem of deciding whose values those should be, and
\citet{sorensen2024} argue for \emph{pluralistic alignment}, in which a
system represents several reasonable value systems rather than one
aggregate. Moral uncertainty is one formally specified way of achieving
this, with explicit credences and an explicit rule in place of an
unexamined average. The inclusion of Ubuntu follows from the same
motive. Non-Western traditions are nearly absent from alignment
research, although \citet{mhlambi2020} argues that Ubuntu's relational
conception of personhood bears directly on AI governance, and
\citeauthor{metz2007}'s reconstruction of Ubuntu as a moral theory
\cite{metz2007} is precise enough to operationalise.

On the evaluation side, ETHICS \cite{hendrycks2020} and Delphi
\cite{jiang2021} score isolated actions rather than select among
competing responses. The dilemmas used here come from DailyDilemmas, a
corpus of everyday quandaries annotated with the values each side
implicates \cite{dailydilemmas2024}. Because the judges are language
models, they inherit that method's known biases, verbosity among them
\cite{zheng2023}, and the axiom harness of
Section~\ref{sec:axiom-validation} therefore tests each judge on pairs
matched so those biases have little to grip.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}


\subsection{Dilemma dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

The dilemmas come from DailyDilemmas, a corpus of everyday moral
quandaries, each posed as a binary choice between a named
\texttt{TO\_DO} action and a named \texttt{NOT\_TO\_DO} action
\cite{dailydilemmas2024}. This choice of two actions gives every candidate response a canonical action to be read
against, which is what makes the two-level measure of
Section~\ref{sec:divergence} possible. In the dataset, each \texttt{TO\_DO} and \texttt{NOT\_TO\_DO} 
also arrives
annotated with the values implicated on either side,

Five hundred dilemmas were drawn from the dataset. The
value annotations served as a rough proxy for tension, with each value
mapped onto utilitarian, deontological, or Ubuntu keyword sets
(Appendix~\ref{app:filter}). A
dilemma was kept only when the three frameworks split across the two
named actions. Survivors were ranked by how evenly the frameworks pull
and how much annotated evidence backs each lean, and the top $500$
retained. Over
half of the distinct values never match a keyword. The mapping is
itself a naive estimate, and the resulting pool is expected to be contested
rather than known to be.

\subsection{Response Generation}
\label{sec:generation}
Sixteen candidate responses per dilemma are sampled from a local
\texttt{llama3.1:8b} model under one neutral prompt
(Appendix~\ref{app:generation}). Priming the
generator with a moral framework would manufacture the very
disagreement the experiment sets out to detect, so the frameworks enter
only at the judging stage and diversity comes from decoding alone,
temperature $1.1$ with nucleus sampling at $p = 0.95$. The temperature
sits deliberately above the conventional range, because the rules can
only be distinguished by a pool that spreads across the options, and the
nucleus cap keeps that heat from degenerating into incoherence.
Generation is therefore hot while scoring is cold (temperature $0.1$,
Section~\ref{sec:axiom-validation}).

A recurring artefact of this stage is refusal. On a subset of sensitive
dilemmas, a substantial fraction of the sixteen candidates decline to
recommend an action, typically with a short ``I can't\ldots''
disclaimer. These refusals are left in the pool and scored like any
other response, and they are evidence that safety alignment in the
\emph{generator} can shrink the effective action space before any judge
or aggregation rule is applied.

\subsection{Reward Models}
\label{sec:judges}

The three judges are the same underlying model, \texttt{gpt-4o-mini},
steered by a system prompt, reproduced verbatim in
Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. Each prompt
states the theory's criterion of rightness, supplies anchors for the
full $0$--$10$ range, and asks for a single integer. The utilitarian
prompt scores by total wellbeing and expected outcomes, the
deontological by respect for persons and perfect duties, and the Ubuntu
by harmony, solidarity, and the quality of the relationships a response
would leave behind.

\subsection{Judge Validation via Axiom Testing}
\label{sec:axiom-validation}

Before the scores can be trusted, it must be shown that each judge
discriminates between responses in the direction its assigned theory
predicts. Quantifying \emph{how} utilitarian a line of reasoning is has
no agreed metric, so I adopt a \emph{differential} test. Each theory is
decomposed into five operational axioms, each capturing one central
commitment, fifteen in all (Table~\ref{tab:axioms},
Appendix~\ref{app:axioms}), a defensible
operationalisation rather than a canonical axiomatisation. The
utilitarian axioms Ut1--Ut5 follow \citeauthor{sidgwick1907}'s
articulation of classical utilitarianism
\cite{bentham1789,mill1863,sidgwick1907}. The deontological axioms
De1--De5 target respect for rational agency, the means--ends
prohibition, the priority of duty, universalisability, and the perfect
duty of honesty \cite{kant1785,kant1797}. The Ubuntu axioms Ub1--Ub5
follow \citeauthor{metz2007}'s reconstruction of the tradition, on which
an action is right insofar as it produces harmony and reduces discord,
alongside its cognate notions of solidarity, restorative justice, and
relational personhood \cite{mbiti1969,metz2007,metz2011,gyekye1997,tutu1999}.

\subsubsection{Test construction and pass criterion}
\label{sec:axiom-results}

Each axiom is tested by three authored dilemmas, $45$ in all, each
presenting a pair of responses constructed to differ \emph{only} along
the dimension the axiom isolates. Pairs are length-matched and
register-matched, so the verbosity and fluency biases of language-model
judges have little to grip. Each pair carries a predicted direction
(\texttt{a > b}, \texttt{b > a}, or null) for all three judges, not
only the axiom's owner, so the off-diagonal cells encode predictions
about where the frameworks should disagree. Responses are graded by the
same judge functions used in the main pipeline, each scored
independently alongside the dilemma it addresses. A predicted cell
passes when the sign of the score difference matches the prediction, an
exact tie failing. Judges score at temperature $0.1$. The objective of this 
harness was to evaluate the judges against these axiom dilemmas to formally certify their fidelity to their respective ethical frameworks

On the first run, two judges failed a couple of tests on their own
axioms (finding these failures was the premise of the axiom test).
Where each judge failed, and how, was used to rewrite the two failing
prompts. The deontological prompt gained a clause making
truthfulness a perfect duty, and the utilitarian
prompt lost a clause that encoded risk-aversion rather than classical
risk-neutrality. The final wording is in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.


A rewrite made after seeing a failure can fit the dilemmas that exposed
it. To address this circularity, prompts were frozen and six new
pairs were written on the same two axioms, three for honesty and three
for expected wellbeing. Section~\ref{sec:held-out} reports those pairs.


After the rewrite, the harness was run five times to absorb the mild
stochasticity. For each judge's preferred response on its own axiom
dilemmas, the diagonal, the mean pass rate comes out to be $99.1\%$, as
seen in Table~\ref{tab:grid}. Each judge therefore discriminates in the
direction its own theory predicts, which is what the harness sets out
to certify.

Alongside the owner's predicted winner, a pair also carries predicted directions
for the other two judges wherever a cross-framework contrast was
expected, so the same pair is put to all three and they are expected to
rank it differently. Twenty of the thirty off-diagonal cells hold in
every run, giving a mean of $68.7\%$ and an overall $86.9\%$ across all
$75$ predicted cells. Two blocks hold outright, the deontological judge
opposing the utilitarian prediction on all six Ut cells where a Kantian
should object, and the Ubuntu judge matching all three De cells.

These off-diagonal directions are only estimates of where the
traditions ought to part company, so a miss is a wrong expectation
about how two theories relate rather than a judge misapplying its own
theory. They are reported as a probe of differentiation, not as a
certification the judges must pass.

One substantive finding deserves emphasis. On the honesty axiom (De5),
the utilitarian judge failed to reward welfare-improving deception on

against the strict-welfarist prediction. Alignment training appears to
instil an honesty guardrail that persists even when the model is
instructed to reason as a strict consequentialist, a limitation worth
bearing in mind whenever aligned LLMs are used as ethical evaluators.

\subsection{Aggregation}
\label{sec:aggregation}

\subsubsection{The aggregation rules}
\label{sec:rules}

For a dilemma with candidate responses $R$, let $c_i(R)$ be the
choiceworthiness of $R$ under framework $i \in \{\text{Ut}, \text{De},
\text{Ub}\}$. Initially, each framework is assigned equal credences $w_i = 1/3$ in the main analysis so that
differences between rules come from the aggregation function rather than
from a prior ranking of the traditions (a sensitivity analysis relaxes
this; Section~\ref{sec:results-credences}). Each rule selects
\[
  R^{\star} = \arg\max_{R}\, f\bigl(c_1(R),\, c_2(R),\, c_3(R)\bigr),
\]
so the four rules differ only in $f$ (Table~\ref{tab:rules}). What is
substituted for $c_i$ is settled in Section~\ref{sec:zscore}. The \textbf{baseline} consults the utilitarian
judge alone, standing in for a system aligned to one tradition.
\textbf{Expected choiceworthiness} averages the three scores
\cite{macaskill2020}, \textbf{maximin} selects the response whose
worst-treated framework fares best, and the \textbf{Nash moral
parliament} maximises the product of each framework's surplus $S_i$
above a disagreement point \cite{nash1950}.

\subsubsection{Global z-scoring}
\label{sec:zscore}

Raw $0$--$10$ scores cannot be aggregated as if they were already
comparable. A judge that spreads its scores widely will swing EC,
Maximin, and Nash more than a judge stuck in a narrow band, which is
the intertheoretic value problem of Section~\ref{sec:background} in
statistical form \cite{macaskill2020}. Each judge $j$'s pooled raw
scores over every dilemma and candidate therefore yield a mean $\mu_j$
and standard deviation $\sigma_j$, and each raw score $x$ is replaced
with $z = (x - \mu_j)/\sigma_j$. This way, a one-point lead no longer counts
for more under a wide-ranging judge than under a compressed one. 
The scope is deliberately \emph{global} rather than per-dilemma.
Per-dilemma $z$-scoring divides by the standard deviation of that
dilemma's sixteen candidates alone, so a judge that rated every
candidate between $8$ and $9$ would be reported as decisively preferring
one of them. 

EC and Maximin use these $z$-scores directly. Nash however needs non-negative
gains above a disagreement point, fixed a priori at a raw score of $0$,
absence of moral worth on the rubric. Subtracting the $z$-score of raw
$0$ from each response's $z$-score simplifies to the surplus
$S_i = X_i / \sigma_i$, where $X_i$ is the raw score under judge $i$.

\subsection{Divergence Measurement}
\label{sec:divergence}


Divergence is measured at the two levels distinguished in the
Introduction: disagreement over the selected text, and disagreement over
the recommended action.

\subsubsection{Recommendation labelling}
\label{sec:labelling}

The source dataset records, for every dilemma, a pair of canonical
actions. Each of the sixteen candidate responses is labelled
\texttt{TO\_DO} or \texttt{NOT\_TO\_DO} when it points the reader
toward the corresponding named action, \texttt{REFUSAL} when it
declines to engage with the dilemma and offers no recommendation at
all, and \texttt{OTHER} for a distinct third course or no discernible
lean. The threshold for the two action labels is deliberately
permissive. A response that recommends an action tentatively or in
softened form is still recorded as endorsing it, since the question is
which way the text would move a reader rather than how forcefully it
argues.


Labels are assigned \emph{post hoc} by a separate model
(\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}, prompt in Appendix~\ref{app:generation}), since
a self-reported label is a claim about intent rather than an
independent reading of the text. Each label is
stored with the short phrase in the response that carries the
recommendation, allowing the labelling to be audited without re-reading
the corpus. The labels have not been validated against human ground
truth, so automatic consistency checks are reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results-labels} and a hand-labelled gold sample is
left to future work.

\subsubsection{Set-valued winners}
\label{sec:ties}




On a $0$--$10$ integer rubric, distinct responses frequently receive
identical score patterns, and because global $z$-scoring is an affine
transform of each judge's raw scores, such ties survive normalisation
intact. Several candidates may therefore share the top value under a given
rule. Rather than breaking ties arbitrarily, each rule's selection is
treated as a \emph{winner set} --- all candidates whose value lies within
$10^{-9}$ of the maximum --- so every reported disagreement is one that no
choice of tie-break could remove. The tolerance only absorbs
floating-point error, since the smallest genuine gap between two
candidates is a raw point, worth at least $0.13$ once normalised.

\subsubsection{Agreement at two levels}
\label{sec:two-levels}

Let $S_A$ and $S_B$ denote the winner sets of rules $A$ and $B$ on a given
dilemma, and let $\ell(\cdot)$ denote the recommendation label of a response.

\paragraph{Response level.}
Rules $A$ and $B$ \emph{can agree} on a response when their winner sets
overlap, $S_A \cap S_B \neq \emptyset$, and \emph{diverge} when the sets
are disjoint. In the disjoint case no tie-breaking convention could
produce a common winner, so the disjoint count is a lower bound on
disagreement. A corresponding upper bound, used only as a robustness
check, is the probability that two uniform draws from $S_A$ and $S_B$
differ.

\paragraph{Recommendation level.}
Rules $A$ and $B$ can agree on an \emph{action} when some label appears in
both winner sets,
$\{\ell(r) : r \in S_A\} \cap \{\ell(r) : r \in S_B\} \neq \emptyset$, and
diverge when the label sets share nothing, as when one winner set is
entirely \texttt{TO\_DO} and the other entirely \texttt{NOT\_TO\_DO}.

Both definitions extend to all four rules at once, which are unanimous
at a level when the intersection over all four winner sets
(respectively, label sets) is non-empty. Response-level unanimity
entails recommendation-level unanimity, and the converse fails. Two
kinds of nominal agreement are counted separately, since a shared
\texttt{REFUSAL} records only that the generator declined to answer,
and a shared \texttt{OTHER} only that both winners depart from the
binary.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}


\subsection{The labelled pool}
\label{sec:results-labels}

Of the $8{,}000$ candidate responses, $3{,}195$ are labelled
\texttt{TO\_DO}, $4{,}074$ \texttt{NOT\_TO\_DO}, $668$ \texttt{REFUSAL},
and $63$ \texttt{OTHER}. Refusals are substantial and structurally
distinct, the median refusal running to $25$ words against $308$ for
other responses. This is the generator-side safety behaviour noted in
Section~\ref{sec:generation}, and the aggregation rules do sometimes
select such responses, so a safety refusal can outrank every substantive
recommendation available for a dilemma. \texttt{OTHER}, by contrast, is
rare ($0.8\%$), a genuine residual rather than a repository for cautious
answers.

Because the labels were produced by a second language model, two
automatic consistency checks were run. Of the supporting quotes stored
with the labels, $99.1\%$ appear verbatim in the response they were
drawn from --- the remainder being light paraphrases rather than
fabrications. Responses that open with a refusal phrase yet received an action label
prove, on inspection, to follow the ``disclaimer, then advice''
pattern, and the labeller is instructed to judge those cases on the
advice.

\subsection{Ties}
\label{sec:results-ties}



Ties at the top are pervasive and markedly uneven across methods. The
utilitarian baseline is indifferent among two or more candidates on
$77\%$ of dilemmas, with a mean tied set of seven and $26$ dilemmas
where its winner set is the entire pool. EC and Nash tie on roughly a
third of dilemmas, Maximin on just over half.

The pattern reflects the scoring scale more than moral indifference. A judge draws on only about four distinct val-
ues across the sixteen candidates for a dilemma. With 16 candidates, 60\% of
them share their exact score triple with a competitor,
and identical triples remain tied after z-scoring. The distributions are also top-heavy, the
ceiling effect anticipated during judge design. The baseline inherits
this coarseness directly, consulting one coordinate.

\subsection{Two levels of agreement}
\label{sec:results-two-levels}

($21\%$) no common text exists, yet every winner set contains the same
core recommendation. On the remaining $56$ ($11.2\%$) the rules diverge on
the action itself.

Taken together, the two lower rows mean the rules fail to converge on a
single response in $161$ of the $500$ dilemmas, or $32\%$. All four
rules read identical scores for identical candidates.

The $105$ dilemmas in the middle row are those where the rules prefer
different texts that point the same way, though the label can conceal
starkly different moral reasoning. Consider the dilemma in which a
struggling artist is offered a large gift of money by a friend, and the
question is whether to accept. The utilitarian baseline selects a
response scored $(8, 3, 5)$ by the utilitarian, deontological, and

the risk to the friendship. EC and Nash select a $(7, 4, 8)$ response

dependence and lost autonomy. The three winner sets are fully disjoint,
though every winner carries \texttt{TO\_DO}. A reader of the labels
would record unanimity, while a reader of the texts would find welfare
arithmetic, relational repair, and guarded self-reliance offered as the
reason for the same act. All three responses are reproduced in
Appendix~\ref{app:cases}.


Nor is that structure rare. In $101$ of these $105$ dilemmas at least
one pair of rules selects winner sets that are fully disjoint yet carry
the same single label. However, distinct texts are also often paraphrases.

\subsection{Why the rules diverge}
\label{sec:results-why}


The $56$ action-level divergences decompose into two families. In $31$
dilemmas the three uncertainty rules share a recommendation and the
baseline stands apart, and in $23$ of these the pattern is identical.
The utilitarian judge favours a response that urges the action, the
other two judges score that response poorly, and the aggregate counsels
restraint. In the remaining $25$ the uncertainty rules split among
themselves, and in $19$ of those Maximin is the sole dissenter. EC and
Nash almost never part company at this level, so the operative choice
among the uncertainty rules is whether to protect the worst-served
framework.



A dilemma from the second family shows the character of Maximin's
dissents. Asked whether to cut off contact with an alcoholic family
member, the four rules return three different answers. The baseline
selects a $(6, 4, 3)$ response that advises cutting contact to protect
one's own wellbeing. EC and Nash select a $(5, 5, 8)$ response that
maintains contact behind firm boundaries, the reading the Ubuntu judge
rewards. Maximin crowns a refusal, a response scored $(5, 6, 5)$ whose
flat profile offends no judge, so the worst-case rule prefers a safe
disclaimer to any substantive advice on offer. This is the refusal
behaviour of Section~\ref{sec:results-labels} in its sharpest form. All
three responses are reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:cases}.

\subsection{When does aggregation matter?}
\label{sec:results-conditional}

\textbf{RESUME PROOF READING HERE}


Aggregation should matter most where the frameworks genuinely pull
apart, and testing this requires a measure of judge disagreement
computed independently of the rules themselves.

Two such measures are used. The first captures conflict of
\emph{ordering}. For each pair of judges $j$ and $k$, let $\tau_b(j,k)$
be the Kendall rank correlation between their scores for the sixteen
responses, corrected for ties. The dilemma's ranking disagreement is
then
%
\begin{equation}
D_{\text{rank}} = 1 - \tfrac{1}{3}\left[\tau_b(u,d) + \tau_b(u,b) + \tau_b(d,b)\right],
\label{eq:drank}
\end{equation}
%
which is zero when all three judges rank the candidates alike, one when
their orderings are unrelated, and larger still when they are opposed.
Appendix~\ref{app:drank} works the measure through on a four-response
example.
The second measure captures conflict of \emph{level}, the mean over
responses of the standard deviation of the three judges' $z$-scores,
$D_{\text{spread}} = \tfrac{1}{16}\sum_i s_i$. Standardised scores are
used so that a lenient judge does not register as a dissenting one. The
two measures are only moderately related, with a Spearman correlation
of $0.40$, so a finding that holds under both is the stronger for it.

Sorting the dilemmas into quartiles by ranking disagreement makes the
answer plain (Table~\ref{tab:conditional}). Action-level divergence sits
flat at six or seven per cent across the three calmer quartiles and then
rises to $27\%$ in the most contested one. That quartile alone accounts
for $33$ of the $56$ dilemmas on which the rules cannot share a
recommendation, and its lower boundary sits at a mean pairwise rank
correlation of about zero, so the rules cease to agree almost exactly
where the frameworks cease to share an ordering. Response-level
divergence follows the same course, from $24\%$ to $53\%$.

The gradient is unlikely to be chance. Mean ranking disagreement is
$0.96$ on the dilemmas where the rules diverge on an action against
$0.74$ where they do not, a difference significant under a one-sided
Mann-Whitney test at $p < 10^{-8}$ with a rank-biserial effect size of
$0.46$. The pattern survives at the response level, under the
level-based measure, where action-level divergence climbs steadily from
$2\%$ to $19\%$, and when the baseline is set aside and only EC,
Maximin, and Nash are compared, where it still roughly triples across
the quartiles.

This is the shape the theory predicts. Had divergence been flat in
judge disagreement, the headline figure of $11.2\%$ would be hard to
distinguish from scoring noise. The relationship is an association
rather than a demonstrated cause, since dilemmas on which the judges
disagree may differ in other respects that also invite divergence.

\subsection{Sensitivity checks}
\label{sec:results-credences}

To test whether the observed agreement depends on the equal-credence
choice, the aggregation stage was re-run twice with a $2{:}1{:}1$ tilt,
favouring deontology and then Ubuntu. Only EC and Nash can respond,
since Maximin protects the worst-served theory whatever its credence
and the baseline consults a single judge.

The tilts move the rules considerably at the level of text, handing EC
a different winner set on up to $85$ of $500$ dilemmas. Almost none of
that movement reaches the action. Action-level disagreement rises from
$56$ dilemmas to $61$ under either tilt, and most dilemmas that lose
their shared response merely prefer a different text pointing the same
way. Doubling a theory's credence changes which justification wins far
more often than what the system recommends, so the headline of
Table~\ref{tab:two-levels} is not an artefact of the equal-credence
choice.

A second check varies the normalisation rather than the credences. The
global $z$-scoring of Section~\ref{sec:zscore} divides each judge by
its own standard deviation, and the substantive alternative divides
every judge by one pooled sigma, so that no judge's spread is stretched
relative to another's. The baseline is monotone-invariant and the Nash
product scale-invariant, so only EC and Maximin can move, and at the
level of text they do, EC selecting a different winner set on $100$ of
the $500$ dilemmas and Maximin on $80$. The two-level table barely
notices. Action-level divergence moves from $56$ dilemmas to $54$, and
only $20$ dilemmas change row at all.

\subsection{Interpretation}
\label{sec:results-read}

On the quantity the motivating premise concerns, the recommended action, the
observed effect is modest. Once justification is set aside, EC and Nash are
close to interchangeable, and replacing a utilitarian default with any of
the three uncertainty rules changes the selected action in fewer than one
dilemma in ten, on a pool already filtered toward moral contestedness.

The result remains informative because it locates \emph{where} the
machinery matters: rarely between one uncertainty rule and another,
occasionally against a single-tradition default, and more often as a
change of justification than of action. Three features of the pipeline
help explain why the action-level figure is small. First, the candidate
pool is homogeneous, since responses come from an $8$B-parameter model
under one neutral prompt, and every rule is often reduced to ranking
stylistic variants of one recommendation. Second, the judges sometimes
saturate, and where the deontological and Ubuntu scores cluster near
the top of the scale, EC, Maximin, and Nash become effectively
utilitarian-driven. Third, the keyword filter of
Section~\ref{sec:dataset} predicts where disagreement should arise but
cannot guarantee it, so part of the pool proved less contested than the
annotations suggested.

None of this licenses substituting the response-level count for the
$56$, which answers a different question and is inflated by ties among
paraphrases. The defensible claim is narrower: modelling moral
uncertainty rarely changes the recommended action relative to other
uncertainty rules, and changes it relative to a utilitarian default in
roughly eight dilemmas in a hundred.

\section{Distilling the Judges}
\label{sec:distil}

Nothing so far has put a moral framework inside a model.
This section asks whether a framework can instead be installed in the
weights, as opposed to using a system prompt each time, by training
three small models. Here we train one small model per judge, grading each with the same
instruments as its teacher.

Success would matter for two reasons. A judge in open weights is a
small, fixed artifact that can be downloaded, run offline, and probed
by anyone, where a prompted judge over a closed model cannot be
inspected or redistributed, and disappears when the provider retires
it. It would also turn the axiom harness of
Section~\ref{sec:axiom-validation} from a one-off validation of this
paper's instrument into a certificate that any third party can re-run
against the weights. Neither claim can be taken on trust, and the
results below show that correlation with the
original judge's scores is the cheapest property to earn.

\subsection{Setup}

\textbf{PROOF READ THREAD 2}

Each copy is \texttt{Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct} with a low-rank adapter
($4.4$M trainable parameters, $0.28\%$ of the model) and a
classification head over the eleven integer scores. It reads a dilemma
and one response in the format its judge saw, and it predicts the score
its judge would give. The training data already existed as the $8{,}000$
scored responses of the main study, split $400/100$ by dilemma. The
sixteen responses to one dilemma are correlated, so a split by response
would leak. Every number below therefore comes from the $1{,}600$
responses of the hundred dilemmas that no copy saw in training.

A classification head was preferred to regression because the judges
act as verdict makers, and a regressor answers a bimodal verdict
distribution with the mean of two verdicts, a score the judge never
gives. An identically configured regression control confirmed the
choice by losing on every measure.

The copies are read against a ladder of references, from the untrained
base model under a generic prompt that names no framework, through the
same model given the full judge prompts, to a ridge regression from
word counts of the response to the teacher's scores. The ceiling is the
teacher's agreement with itself, measured by re-scoring $208$ of the
held-out responses with the original judges.

the ten-point scale. The training lift is easiest to see on the

the deontological judge hardly at all, so the deontological judge is
the most framework-specific instrument of the three, and the hardest to
copy.

\subsection{Substitution into the pipeline}

Re-running normalisation and aggregation on the held-out dilemmas with
the copies in place of the judges separates what survives from what
does not. Winner identity does not survive. For the three rules that
combine all judges, the copy pipeline's winner set shares a response
with the teacher's in only $43$ to $59\%$ of dilemmas. The single-judge
Baseline reaches $86\%$, since errors cannot compound across judges.
The recommended action largely survives and is shared in $91$ to $99\%$
of dilemmas. The structure of the disagreement survives almost exactly.
The copy pipeline reproduces each rule-versus-rule divergence rate
within a few points, and it seats all four rules on one response in
$72\%$ of dilemmas against the teacher's $71$. The word-count floor
fails this last test, deflating unanimity to $56\%$ and inflating every
divergence rate. Structure preservation therefore discriminates between
copies where correlation does not, and a study run on the copies would
report every aggregate finding of Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{The axiom exam}

The axioms of Section~\ref{sec:axiom-validation} supply $51$ scored
pairs, the $45$ originals and the six confirmation pairs. The
distinction between the two sets matters only for the teacher, whose
prompts were revised after failures on the originals. The copies never
saw any pair in any form, so for them all $51$ are one exam. They pass
$67$ of the $81$ directional predictions and $43$ of the $51$
own-framework cells, with the diagonal pattern of Table~\ref{tab:grid}.
The teacher passes $99\%$ of its own-framework cells on the originals
and $90\%$ on the confirmation pairs. The word counter manages $28$ of
$51$ with no diagonal pattern at all.

The instructive comparison is with the untrained base model. Given a
single sentence naming the framework, base Qwen already passes $38$ of
the $51$ own-framework cells, so training adds five pairs, and most of
what this exam measures was in the model before distillation began. The
clearest training gain sits off the diagonal, in predictions about how
the other two frameworks order a pair, where the copies pass $24$ of
$30$ against the base model's $12$. The margins complete the picture.
The teacher separates the members of an axiom pair by $5.6$ points on
average, the copies by $1.5$, and on one confirmation pair the teacher
scores $10$ against $2$ where its copy scores $6.6$ against $7.3$, a
reversal. Axiom responses run to a sentence or two, far from the long
responses of the training set, and the copies' verdicts soften exactly
there.

Taken together, the copies are faithful in aggregate and unreliable in
detail. What training installed was the teacher's scoring habits rather
than the framework itself, and no single measure would have shown it.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

Five limitations point to concrete extensions.

\emph{The measurement scale is coarse.} The tie rates of
Section~\ref{sec:results-ties} are its most visible consequence, and
much of the observed indifference is an artefact of resolution. A wider
rubric would resolve only rounding ties, and a rewritten rubric is a
new instrument that must be revalidated before any comparison.

\emph{The recommendation labels require human validation.} The
automatic checks establish that the labeller is consistent with its own
prompt, and they do not establish that a human would assign the same
labels. A stratified gold sample, labelled blind to the model's output,
would give per-class precision and an estimate of corpus accuracy.

\emph{A categorical label discards degree.} ``Gradually distance
yourself'' and ``cut off contact immediately'' may share a
\texttt{NOT\_TO\_DO} tag while differing sharply in force. A scalar
lean recorded alongside the category would let divergence be graded
rather than binary.

\emph{The homogeneity of the candidate pool is the binding constraint},
as Section~\ref{sec:results-read} argues. A larger generator, or a
procedure designed to produce genuinely opposed courses of action,
would give the rules materially more to discriminate between, and would
settle whether the present figure is a fact about the rules or an
artefact of sampling.

\emph{The distilled copies show what a certificate must contain.} A
certification suite for distilled judges would hold out a larger bank
of pairs written after every prompt and training decision is frozen,
grade margins as well as directions, and test within-dilemma ranking
against a measured ceiling. Training on rationales rather than bare
scores might also close the gap between copying a judge's habits and
learning its principles.

\section{Conclusion}

This dissertation asked whether differing moral aggregation methods
produce measurably different behaviour when a language-model system
must select among candidate responses to everyday moral dilemmas. It
does, and the pattern of the difference is as informative as its size.
Under conditions engineered to favour agreement, the four rules part
company over which text to present in a third of dilemmas and over what
to recommend in one dilemma in nine, and this is a strict lower bound that no
tie-breaking convention could reduce. Furthermore, the disagreement is not spread
evenly. It concentrates where the three framework judges genuinely rank
the candidates differently, reaching $27\%$ in the most contested
quartile. This is the behaviour an account of moral uncertainty
predicts; aggregation is close to immaterial where the traditions
concur, and it begins to decide the answer precisely where they part.

How the rules diverge matters as much as how often. Swapping a
utilitarian default for a rule that models uncertainty usually changes
which answer a system gives while leaving the advice intact. Where the
advice does change, the aggregate declines the decisive or irreversible
option the utilitarian judge favours. Among the uncertainty rules
themselves, the practical choice is whether to protect the worst-served
tradition, since EC and the Nash bargain rarely disagree about
what to do. Maximin is what that protection costs, as the response
no framework scores badly is often one that none scores well.

The distillation experiment adds a caution for anyone who would take
such judges off the shelf. The three framework judges can be copied
into small open models faithfully enough to reproduce every aggregate
conclusion of this study, yet the copies still fail as judges,
capturing less than half of the teacher's reproducible ranking and
little of the frameworks' principles beyond what their base model
already knew. A correlation that would conventionally certify them
conceals both failures, and behavioural tests of the kind used here,
axioms read against a floor and a ceiling, are the certificate that
matters.

Whose ethics a system serves depends on the rule that combines the
represented values as much as on the values themselves. That rule
deserves to be an explicit, tested design choice rather than an
accident of averaging.

\section{Dilemma Selection Detail}
\label{app:filter}

The source corpus contains $2{,}720$ rows describing $1{,}360$ complete
dilemmas, each row one side of a binary choice together with the values
annotated as backing that side. Every annotated value is mapped to at
most one framework by case-insensitive substring match against the
keyword sets below, and matches are counted on each side. A dilemma
passes the gate only when at least one framework prefers each named
action, which $579$ dilemmas do. These are ranked by balance, tie-broken
by confidence, and the top $500$ retained.

For a framework with $t$ matched values behind \texttt{TO\_DO} and $n$
behind \texttt{NOT\_TO\_DO}, its lean is $(t - n)/(t + n + 1)$, a signed
value in $[-1, 1]$ whose smoothing constant keeps thin evidence
tentative. A dilemma's \emph{balance} is $1 - |\text{mean lean}|$ over
the three frameworks, so that $1$ is perfect deadlock, and its
\emph{confidence} is the total number of matched values, which ranges
from $2$ to $24$ in the gated set.

\paragraph{Utilitarian keywords.} {\small wellbeing, well-being,
welfare, happiness, satisfaction, survival, health, safety, comfort,
relief, peace, peace of mind, efficiency, productivity, profit,
profitability, financial stability, financial security, job security,
stability, protection, prosperity, outcome, harm reduction,
resilience.}

\paragraph{Deontological keywords.} {\small honesty, integrity,
fairness, justice, lawfulness, duty, accountability, responsibility,
transparency, respect for rules, professionalism, professional
integrity, professional responsibility, trust, trustworthiness,
respect, dignity, autonomy, right to life, right to privacy, right to
information, right to autonomy, respect for privacy, respect for
autonomy, respect for life, freedom, freedom of expression, consent,
truth, open communication, discipline.}

\paragraph{Ubuntu keywords.} {\small compassion, empathy, solidarity,
unity, teamwork, cooperation, support, care, concern, generosity, love,
loyalty, kinship, community, togetherness, selflessness, sacrifice,
understanding, tolerance, patience, guidance, consideration, respect
for diversity, respect for others, friendship, mutual aid.}

\section{Generation and Labelling Detail}
\label{app:generation}

Candidate responses were generated by \texttt{llama3.1:8b} served
locally through Ollama, sixteen per dilemma at temperature $1.1$ with
nucleus sampling at $p = 0.95$, and with no system prompt. Each request
contained the dilemma text verbatim followed by a single neutral ask:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=prompt]
Give a clear recommendation and explain your reasoning.
\end{lstlisting}

Refusals cluster heavily. On three dilemmas, concerning stealing
supplies when one would not get caught, giving up children for
adoption, and reacting to a friend's sexual orientation, at least
fifteen of the sixteen candidates refused. A typical example reads ``I
cannot provide a recommendation on theft. Is there anything else I can
help with?''.

The recommendation labels of Section~\ref{sec:labelling} were produced
by \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} under the system prompt on the next page, with
the two named actions and the response to label supplied in the user
message.

\newpage
\begin{longpromptbubble}{Recommendation labeller}{labdark}{lablight}
You label which course of action a response to a moral dilemma steers the reader toward.

Each dilemma is a binary choice between two named actions, supplied to you as TO_DO and NOT_TO_DO. You are not judging whether the response is good, well-argued or ethical — only which way it points.

Apply these steps in order and stop at the first that applies.

STEP 1 — REFUSAL
Use REFUSAL only when the response declines to engage with the dilemma at all. Typical marks: an "I can't help with that" or "I'm not able to advise on this" disclaimer, a bare redirection to a professional offered *instead of* an answer, or an explicit statement that it will not take a position and nothing further.
A response that reasons about the dilemma and arrives at any recommendation is NEVER a REFUSAL, however cautiously it is phrased. A disclaimer followed by substantive advice is not a REFUSAL — judge it on the advice.

STEP 2 — TO_DO or NOT_TO_DO
Otherwise, decide which of the two named actions the response points toward, and set the bar LOW. Conviction is not required; a tentative lean is still a lean. Label TO_DO or NOT_TO_DO when the response does any of the following:
  - suggests rather than insists ("you might consider", "it may be best to", "perhaps")
  - recommends the action but hedges it with caveats, conditions or warnings
  - recommends a softened, partial, gradual or staged version of the named action
  - weighs both sides at length but closes by favouring one
  - recommends a step whose evident purpose is the named action's goal, even if it words the step differently
  - recommends the action only after a precondition ("talk to them first, and if that fails, ...") — label the action it ultimately settles on
Ask yourself: after reading this, which way would the reader most likely act? Label that side.

STEP 3 — OTHER
Use OTHER only when step 2 genuinely fails. That means either:
  - the response recommends a distinct third course that is not a softer, partial or staged version of either named action (e.g. bringing in a mediator when the choice is between confronting and staying silent), or
  - it lays out both sides so evenly that there is no discernible lean whatsoever.
Do NOT use OTHER for a mild, hesitant, conditional or qualified recommendation. That belongs in STEP 2. OTHER is a residual category and should be rare.

Reply with JSON only, in the form:
{"label": "TO_DO" | "NOT_TO_DO" | "REFUSAL" | "OTHER", "evidence": "<short quote from the response that carries the recommendation, or empty string for REFUSAL>"}
\end{longpromptbubble}

\section{Worked Example of \texorpdfstring{$D_{\text{rank}}$}{D-rank}}
\label{app:drank}

The ranking disagreement of Equation~\ref{eq:drank} is computed over
the sixteen candidates of a dilemma. The four-response example below is
small enough to check by hand. Each judge scores every response on the
raw $0$--$10$ rubric.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\caption{An illustrative dilemma with four candidate responses.}
\label{tab:drank-example}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l c c c@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Response} & \textbf{Utilitarian} & \textbf{Deontological} & \textbf{Ubuntu} \\
\midrule
$R_1$ & 8 & 4 & 9 \\
$R_2$ & 6 & 7 & 8 \\
$R_3$ & 5 & 6 & 4 \\
$R_4$ & 3 & 9 & 5 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Only the orderings matter. The utilitarian judge ranks
$R_1 \succ R_2 \succ R_3 \succ R_4$, the deontological judge
$R_4 \succ R_2 \succ R_3 \succ R_1$, and the Ubuntu judge
$R_1 \succ R_2 \succ R_4 \succ R_3$. Each pair of judges is then
compared over the $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ pairs of responses, and $\tau_b$ is
the number of pairs the two judges order alike minus the number they
order oppositely, divided by six.

The utilitarian and deontological judges agree on one pair and differ
on five, giving $\tau_b(u,d) = (1-5)/6 = -0.67$. The utilitarian and
Ubuntu judges agree on five and differ on one, so
$\tau_b(u,b) = +0.67$, their only disagreement being whether $R_3$ beats
$R_4$. The deontological and Ubuntu judges agree on two and differ on
four, so $\tau_b(d,b) = -0.33$. Substituting into
Equation~\ref{eq:drank},
%
\[
D_{\text{rank}} = 1 - \tfrac{1}{3}\left[-0.67 + 0.67 - 0.33\right]
                = 1.11 .
\]
%
A value above one means the judges are opposed on average rather than
merely unrelated, and this dilemma would fall in the most contested
quartile of Table~\ref{tab:conditional}. No two responses share a score
under any judge here, so $\tau_b$ reduces to the ordinary Kendall
$\tau$. In the real data ties are common, and the $b$ correction
discounts pairs a judge leaves unordered so that a judge scoring
everything alike cannot register as agreement.
